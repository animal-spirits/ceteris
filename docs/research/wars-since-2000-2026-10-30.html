Wars since 2000, and how they can happen on their own

A catalogue of the armed conflicts of 2000 to 2025, the regularities they share, and a proposal for producing them from simulated state alone, with no dates, names or scripted events (ADR 0241).

Summary

  • Most organized violence since 2000 is civil war, increasingly internationalized. UCDP counted 61 state-based conflicts in 2024, the most since 1946, and 65 in 2025, of which 8 were interstate, also a record. Deaths in 2025 (about 245,000) were the highest since 1994.
  • Wars start when a fast trigger lands on slow risk. Coups, revolutions, contested successions, mass-casualty attacks, subsidy cuts, shifts in relative power and the withdrawal of a sponsor fire on states that already carry exclusion, weak capacity, rents, rough terrain and a recent war.
  • Ten causal pathways cover every conflict in the catalogue. Five need mechanisms Ceteris does not have yet: cohesion of the security forces, third-party intervention, armed groups that cross borders, attribution of attacks, and conflict state at 1 January 2000.
  • Proposal: the hazard proposes, actors decide. A calibrated crisis hazard (MID and UCDP minor-conflict rates) creates crises with named actors and a stake; actors escalate or settle through the bargaining model of 11.2 and the new intervention rule. Aggregate onset rates must reproduce the 0110 hazard (a nesting test), so organic does not mean uncalibrated.
  • 1.0 has no conflicts at all today (war is 2.0, ADR 0136). In a calendar-blind run that drops war-driven oil, refugee and capital shocks from the economy. The cheapest fix is a 1.0 conflict-incidence hazard with economic effects only.

Regularities

These are the patterns a simulated 2000-2024 should reproduce in distribution, never by date.

  1. Type mix. Civil conflict dominates counts; interstate war is rare but rising (8 interstate conflicts in 2025). Internationalized civil wars, rare before 2010, became common after 2013.
  2. Interstate wars are short, limited and local. They involve neighbours, a territorial claim or a frozen line, and an unequal balance. Nuclear dyads stay at strikes and skirmishes (India-Pakistan four times, Israel-Iran).
  3. Fast triggers on slow risk. Almost every onset has a dated political trigger: a coup, revolution, disputed election, assassination, mass-casualty attack, subsidy cut, or a neighbour's collapse.
  4. The conflict trap. Most onsets are recurrences or continuations of older conflicts (Chechnya, Karabakh, Gaza, Darfur, Ukraine 2014 to 2022). Peace-years is the strongest single predictor.
  5. Splits inside the state. Armies that are ethnically stacked or paired with a rival paramilitary break apart under stress (Syria, Libya, South Sudan, Sudan, Myanmar). Coups cluster regionally (Sahel 2020-23).
  6. Contagion. Collapse exports fighters and arms (Libya to Mali), sanctuary links neighbours (Syria and Iraq, Afghanistan and Pakistan), and one war pulls in others (Gaza to Lebanon, Yemen, Iran).
  7. Sponsors set duration and outcome. External support lengthens civil wars. When sponsors are stretched or leave, clients collapse within weeks (Kabul 2021, Damascus 2024).
  8. Collapse is nonlinear. Long stalemates end in sudden cascades of desertion and surrender, not gradual attrition.
  9. Great-power regime change produces long insurgencies (Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya). Victory in the first phase says little about the second.
  10. Few decisive endings. Victories are rare (Sri Lanka 2009, Karabakh 2023, Taliban 2021, HTS 2024). Ceasefires that freeze a line are the modal end, and frozen lines thaw when the balance shifts.
  11. Rents finance the fighting. Oil (ISIS, Libya), gold (Sudan, Sahel, Wagner), minerals (DRC), drugs (Afghanistan, Colombia, Mexico), extortion and diaspora money.
  12. Economic footprint. Theatre GDP falls 20 to 60 percent; displacement reaches tens of millions; spillovers run through oil (Libya 2011), gas and grain (2022), chokepoints (Red Sea 2024), sanctions and reserve freezes, and neighbours' defence budgets.
  13. Cheap precision shifts the balance. Drones and missiles let weaker parties strike far (Houthis, Ukraine, Azerbaijan), making cost exchange a production question (ADR 0152).

What Ceteris has today

In place (all 2.0, P6 and P7)

  • 11.2 crisis bargaining (Fearon range, commitment, indivisibility, nuclear cost term)
  • 11.6 civil-war risk; 10.8 protest cascade, stress index, coups
  • 0110 reduced-form onset and termination hazard as the lab default
  • 0069 armed groups and their finance; 0060 organized crime
  • 0073 proliferation and frozen conflicts; 0152 escalation ladder
  • 0145-0154, 0245, 0246 conduct and occupation; 0165 war economy; 0045 sanctions
  • 0051 fat-tailed severity, 0241 calendar-blind runs, 0242 hazard pattern

Gaps found

  • G1. No cohesion or factions in security forces, so P2 and P3 cannot happen and collapses cannot cascade.
  • G2. No rule for who intervenes in someone else's war; sponsors exist only as money in 0069.
  • G3. Armed groups are tied to one state; no branches across borders, no leakage of arms after collapse.
  • G4. Attacks carry no origin, so they cannot cause retaliation.
  • G5. 0110's hazard says where violence starts but not who fights or why; 11.2 says why but has no rate at which disputes arise. Neither alone gives organic, calibrated wars.
  • G6. The pack has no conflict state at 1 January 2000: ongoing wars, armed groups, foreign deployments, frozen lines and claims.
  • G7. 1.0 has no conflicts, so a calendar-blind 1.0 run lacks war shocks to oil, refugees and capital.

Proposal: the hazard proposes, actors decide

Five layers. Nothing in them names a country or a date; every war carries its causal chain in the event log so the chronicle (0049) can show why it happened.

L0
Structural risk, slow state already computed: income and growth, exclusion, state capacity and reach, terrain, rents, youth unemployment, peace-years, neighbourhood conflict; per dyad claims, capability ratio and trend, alliance credibility, trade dependence, nuclear status, regime type.
L1
Triggers, fast events from hazards that exist or are planned: coup and protest cascade (10.8), leader death and succession (0066), elections, price shocks that force subsidy cuts (econ), attacks with attribution (G4), a neighbour's collapse, a sponsor's withdrawal or overstretch, a power-shift window.
L2
Crisis generator. One hazard in the 0051 library creates crises, not wars. A crisis has typed actors (state, faction, group, sponsor) and a stake (territory, regime, autonomy, policy, retaliation). Rate is L0 risk times L1 trigger multipliers. Fitted on MID and ICB crises (interstate) and UCDP minor-conflict onsets (domestic). Drawn by thinning on shared uniforms (0241).
L3
Escalation. Actors bargain under 11.2 with private information and commitment problems; third parties run the intervention rule (G2); factions run the cohesion rule (G1). Outcomes: settle, standoff, limited clash, war. In the lab profile an escalation logit on the same covariates replaces bargaining.
L4
Conduct and termination as designed (0145-0154, 11.8). Sponsor budgets are finite across theatres, so overstretch can collapse a client elsewhere. Cohesion cascades give sudden collapses.
L5
Aftermath. Peace-years reset; ceasefire lines become frozen conflicts with a thaw hazard (0073); armed groups persist with their rents; grievance, destroyed capital and refugees feed L0 again. This is the conflict trap, with no separate term.

New mechanisms

M1. Security-force cohesion

Each state's forces are one to a few factions (regular army, presidential guard, paramilitary), each with an identity profile from the officer corps and a loyalty stock. Loyalty falls with pay arrears (fiscal stress), orders to repress the faction's own group, losses, and leader illegitimacy; it rises with rents shared and sponsor support. Defection is a threshold cascade, the same model as 10.8 protest. A split creates a new armed actor with its units and the cells it holds. Coup-proofing (stacking, parallel forces) lowers coup risk and raises split risk. Calibrated on Powell-Thyne and Cline coups and on defection episodes.

M2. Third-party intervention

Each month, states with a stake in a crisis or war score modes (arms and money, advisers or private forces, air power, ground troops) by value (client tie, basing, kin, refugee inflow, attacks originating there, proliferation risk, commodity exposure) against cost (projection distance, casualty sensitivity by regime type, sanctions, other commitments). Sponsor budgets are shared across theatres. Calibrated on UCDP External Support and International Military Intervention data.

M3. Cross-border armed groups

Groups can open branches in adjacent provinces with low state reach and kin or ideological links. When a state collapses, a share of its arms stock leaks to groups within reach. Groups split and merge through 0189's emergent organizations. Calibrated on UCDP actor histories.

M4. Attribution

Every attack from 0069 records its group and the group's host state. A mass-casualty attack multiplies the target state's crisis rate against the host for some months; the size of the multiplier rises with deaths and rivalry and falls with nuclear deterrence.

M5. Conflict state at t0

The 2000 pack records as state: conflicts active in 1999 (UCDP), armed groups and the cells they control, foreign deployments, ceasefire lines (Karabakh, Transnistria, Abkhazia, South Ossetia, Cyprus, Kashmir, Korea, Western Sahara, Golan) and claims (ICOW). This is 0241's commitments-in-force rule applied to war.

M6. 1.0 conflict incidence

Until 2.0, a reduced-form 0110 hazard per province with economic incidence only: capital destruction, lost output, displacement under 6.5, oil and mineral supply cuts, and shipping premia near chokepoints. No actors, territory or borders change. 2.0 replaces it with L2-L5 and must reproduce its marginal frequencies.

Calibration and tests

  • Nesting. With M1-M4 on and default parameters, simulated onset rates by type and region match the 0110 hazard within its interval. The structural layers explain who and why; they must not change how often.
  • Frequencies, never dates. Onsets per decade by type, interstate share, internationalized share, duration distribution, termination type shares (UCDP Conflict Termination), recurrence share, fatality power law (0051), displacement per battle death.
  • Pathway coverage. Over 32 members and 25 years from the 2000 pack, each of P1-P10 fires at least once, and none exceeds three times its historical share.
  • Ablation. Turning off a trigger family removes its pathway (no attribution, no P6; no cohesion, no P2 or P3) and leaves the others within error.
  • Hold-out. From the 2000, 2005 and 2010 origins, counts and type mix in 2011-2024 against UCDP; history's percentile (0118).
  • Economic footprint. Theatre GDP losses against synthetic-control estimates; refugee flows against UNHCR; oil supply losses against EIA outage data.
