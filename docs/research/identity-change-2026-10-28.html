Design note · population and politics · 28 October 2026

Identity change in Ceteris

How culture, language and religion change over time, how states push them, and how mass atrocities arise and what they leave behind. Grounded in DESIGN 6.4, ADRs 0055, 0058, 0154 and 0189, and in the demographic and genocide-studies literature.

Status: proposal, no ADR yet. Proposed numbers 0223 to 0226 (main is through 0222, PR #59 holds 0208 and 0209).

Short answer

Treat identity as slow-moving pop state with four separate engines, each measured against data. Paradox games spend a currency to flip a province; Ceteris should move shares at rates people have actually measured, so most change comes from births, deaths and moves rather than conversion.

Composition
Groups grow at different rates through fertility, mortality and migration. This is most of the observed change in religion since 2010, and it comes free in 1.0 once P4 keys pops by ethnic group.
Language
Each pop holds a repertoire per heritage language: monolingual, bilingual, or shifted. Children inherit with a probability set by status, schooling and local share. Adults add the dominant language for wages. The Abrams-Strogatz model is the limiting case.
Religion
Affiliation shares plus a religiosity intensity. Intergenerational retention, adult switching (ADR 0058) and cohort secularization (ADR 0055) act on them. Calibrated on Wittgenstein Centre and Pew estimates.
Boundaries
The EPR group list is fixed, but people can re-identify between existing groups, and identity salience (0058) moves with threat, elections and cueing.
Atrocities
Three layers: structural risk (Harff), wartime escalation and restraints (Straus, Valentino), then the act as an order (0154). Consequences run through existing modules. The engine records acts; legal labels come from courts or cited bodies.

1.0 composition only, shares within pops frozen at 2000 (ADR 0207). 2.0-alpha language, religion, boundaries, policy levers, atrocity risk. 2.0-beta atrocity orders, expulsion, consequences. 3.0 new groups forming, pre-2000 history as live state.

Identity state per pop

The pop key stays (province, ethnic group, education, labour status). Everything else is a small vector inside the pop, updated yearly except salience, which updates monthly.

Cost: about 30 floats per pop and a yearly pass over roughly 25,000 (province, group) cells. Under 0.2 ms per monthly tick amortized, against a 35 ms budget.

1. Composition: the demographic engine

Most identity change in the 2000-2025 window is demographic, not conversion. Pew's 2025 study of 2010-2020 finds Muslims grew fastest mainly through a young age structure and higher fertility, while Christianity's share fell mainly through people leaving it in a few regions. Hout, Greeley and Wilde (2001) found that fertility differences explain most of the long shift from mainline to conservative Protestant churches in the United States.

Ceteris gets this for free once P4 keys pops by ethnic group: group-specific fertility (through education, income, urbanization in 6.2), mortality, and migration (6.5) move the national mix while shares inside each pop stay fixed. That is the only identity dynamic in 1.0, and it is already in the plan.

One data change is worth making in P4: initialize religion shares from the Wittgenstein Centre religion-by-age-and-sex estimates (CC BY 4.0) instead of the CIA Factbook's national totals, so that age structure differences between religious groups are present from the start.

2. Language: repertoires and shift

A share vector over all languages cannot represent the usual path of shift, which runs through bilingualism over two or three generations (Fishman 1991; Alba et al. 2002 find Spanish rarely survives into the third generation in the United States). So each pop carries, for its heritage language H and the dominant language D of its state, three shares: H only, bilingual HD, and D only. Groups with no heritage language distinct from D skip the field. Languages come from CLDR territory data and Glottolog codes.

Transmission at birth

P(child acquires H) = h + b · τ τ = σ( k0 + k_s (s_H − s_D) + k_x logit(x_H) + k_e school_H + k_m homogamy + k_f backlash )

Here s is language status (official use, share of income earned in it, media), x_H is the local share of H speakers in the province, school_H is 1 when H is a medium of instruction, homogamy is the in-group marriage rate, and backlash is the salience term below. The child acquires D from schooling and exposure.

Adult change

H → HD : rate α · (1 − x_H) · wage premium of D (Chiswick-Miller) HD → D : rate β · (1 − s_H) · (1 − x_H)^a (attrition)

With no bilinguals and status fixed, the population share of H obeys Abrams and Strogatz (2003), dx/dt = c(1−x)x^a s − c x(1−x)^a(1−s), with a ≈ 1.31 across 42 languages. That model always ends in extinction of one language. The bilingual extension (Mira and Paredes 2005; Minett and Wang 2008) allows stable coexistence when status is high enough or the state supports the minority language, which matches Catalan, Welsh and Basque in the data.

Calibration and validation

Census and survey series with repeated language questions: Welsh (2001, 2011, 2021), Irish, Basque (sociolinguistic surveys 1991-2021, where schooling raised young speakers), Catalan, Quebec French, Russian in Latvia and Estonia, Kazakh in Kazakhstan, Spanish in the US ACS by generation, and Ukraine. Ukraine is the strongest in-window test: surveys show adults switching everyday use toward Ukrainian after 2014 and much faster after 2022, a salience-driven shift that the bilingual stock makes possible.

3. Assimilation policy and backlash

States move these rates through levers that already exist as policy variables or are cheap to add. The sign of each effect is not always the obvious one, which is why they belong in the lab.

Backlash is a single term: when a policy targets a group's identity, that group's salience (0058) rises and raises τ, so coercion can slow the shift it is meant to speed. Its weight is a register parameter with Fouka's estimate as the prior and a lane that sets it to zero.

4. Religion: transmission, switching, secularization

Each pop carries affiliation shares and a religiosity intensity between 0 and 1. Separating the two follows Voas (2009): much of European secularization runs through "fuzzy fidelity", affiliated people whose practice fades before their children stop affiliating.

  • Transmission. Children take parents' affiliation with a retention probability that rises with parental religiosity and religious homogamy. Voas and Crockett (2005) find two religious parents pass on religiosity about half the time and one parent about a quarter.
  • Adult switching. The 0058 hazard, raised by rural-to-urban migration and weak public services (Pentecostal growth in Africa and Latin America), stays behind switch S20.
  • Secularization. Religiosity of each cohort forms from existential security through the 0055 value scores (Norris and Inglehart 2004), then drifts slowly. Voas and Chaves (2016) show US decline is cohort-driven, which this reproduces.
  • State levers. State religion, religious education, restrictions on minority worship (Pew Government Restrictions Index for calibration), and coerced conversion, which counts as a forced-assimilation act.

Targets: Wittgenstein Centre religion estimates and projections (CC BY 4.0, confirmed), Pew 2010-2020 change by country, European Social Survey for attendance by cohort, Latinobarómetro for Protestant growth.

5. Group boundaries and salience

Constructivist work shows that which identity matters, and sometimes which group people report, shifts with politics: Posner (2005) on Zambia, where language groups mattered nationally and tribes locally; Eifert, Miguel and Posner (2010) on ethnic identification rising near competitive elections; Nagel (1995) on American Indian ethnic renewal in the US census. Ukraine 2001-2022 again shows self-identification moving between existing groups.

Proposal: keep the EPR group list fixed for the run and allow re-identification flows between existing groups at a slow rate driven by salience, status gaps and intermarriage. New groups forming (CK3-style divergence) needs a timescale and data the 2000-2100 window does not have, so it waits for the deep-history work in 3.0 (ADR 0131). Continuous culture drift is already ADR 0055.

6. Mass atrocities

This section is about representing the causes, warning signs and consequences of the worst things states and armed groups do, at the fidelity a researcher would need. It is not a mechanic to win with. ADR 0154 already decided that such acts are orders open to player and AI with full consequences; this proposal adds the structural model behind AI behaviour and the slow consequences that 0154 leaves out.

Onset is a logit hazard per (state, target group, year), estimated on episode data the way ADR 0110 does conflict. Once begun, intensity follows a persistence process that ends through military outcome, intervention, regime change or exhaustion, calibrated on UCDP one-sided violence durations. The Early Warning Project publishes annual out-of-sample risk rankings; matching or beating their ranking accuracy is the forecast test, scored like the other benchmarks in ADR 0052.

Consequences, through modules that already exist

  • Demography. Deaths by age and sex profile per act type, displacement into the 6.5 hazard flow, and lost cohorts that echo in fertility for decades.
  • Economy. Assets of the target group transfer to the perpetrator state or group (an ownership edit, 0115 and 0201), and their human capital leaves. Acemoglu, Hassan and Robinson (2011) find Russian cities that lost their Jewish middle class in the Holocaust grew more slowly and voted differently decades later.
  • Trust and identity. Rohner, Thoenig and Zilibotti (2013) find ethnic violence in Uganda lowered trust and raised ethnic identity. Lupu and Peisakhin (2017) find descendants of Crimean Tatars deported in 1944 still hold stronger group identity three generations later. These set generalized trust (0056) and salience (0058) for survivors and their children.
  • Politics and law. Legitimacy at home and abroad, sanctions and alliance hazards, referrals and cases in the courts module (0072), and intervention, which since 2000 has been rare.
  • Territory. Expulsion changes who lives where. Settlement weights update from the pops, so the map of groups changes without any separate edit.

Presentation rules

  • The engine records acts, counts and evidence of intent. It never applies the word genocide itself; legal labels come from simulated court outcomes or, for history, from the cited body, the same stance as ADR 0222 on disputed borders.
  • No score, objective, achievement, replay hook or music cue ever rewards or highlights an atrocity. The run score (0132, 0129) counts the deaths against the perpetrator.
  • An atrocity order shows its expected cost screen before it is confirmed, like other high-cost orders.
  • A read-only atrocity risk map mode shows the structural hazard by state and group, with its factors, as a warning-signs lens. The lab question it serves is prevention: which levers lower risk.
  • Identity is shown as statistics and map modes only (0141). Groups keep their data names.

The pattern: change is a player expenditure or a uniform rate, with no bilingual stage, no generational transmission, no backlash and no measured rates. The historical titles leave atrocities out; only Stellaris includes them, against fictional species. Ceteris has already chosen inclusion for real groups (0154), so its justification has to be fidelity: calibrated causes, rates and consequences, plainly presented.
